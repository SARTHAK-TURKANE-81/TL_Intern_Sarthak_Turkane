Introduction

Week 2 marked the shift from digital design foundations into full digital fabrication — pairing hands-on training on laser cutting and vinyl/iron-on machines with project ideation, one-on-one mentorship, and a guest lecture on design thinking that reshaped how I approach building anything from scratch.

Week 2 of the Summer Internship Program turned out to be one of the most action-packed and genuinely exciting weeks so far — a blend of Project Ideation, Industry Mentorship, Design Thinking, and hands-on time with Digital Fabrication Technologies. The week opened with mentorship sessions that helped shape the direction of my own project, before moving into the machines and software that turn digital designs into real, physical products.

On the fabrication side, the week introduced Computer-Controlled Manufacturing through two very different but complementary technologies: CO₂ Laser Cutting using LaserCAD, and Vinyl & Iron-On Cutting using Cricut Design Space. Alongside these, an introduction to the Bambu Lab 3D Printing Ecosystem rounded out the picture — covering additive, subtractive, and cutting-based fabrication all within the same lab.

A real highlight of the week was a guest lecture on Design Thinking and Rapid Prototyping, which reframed how I think about building anything — starting from empathy and a clearly defined problem, rather than jumping straight to a solution. Being asked about my own project during the session, and getting to talk through the Computer Vision and Embedded Systems ideas I'm working on, was honestly one of the most exciting moments of the week.

Rather than treating each tool in isolation, the week emphasized the complete Digital-to-Physical Workflow — how an idea moves from a sketch, through Vector-Based Design and Machine Parameter Configuration, into a finished object using the right material and the right machine for the job.

Building on the Week 1 Digital Design Foundations, this week showed just how much can be built when Mentorship, Design Thinking, and Fabrication Technology come together — and set the stage for the project work ahead.

ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING & 3D PRINTING SYSTEMS

3D Printing Ecosystem — Bambu Lab A1 Mini & X2D

With laser cutting and vinyl cutting covering subtractive and cutting-based fabrication, the next piece of the puzzle was Additive Manufacturing — and the lab's Bambu Lab 3D Printing Ecosystem turned out to be the perfect introduction. This session focused on understanding two machines that sit at opposite ends of the lineup: the compact Bambu Lab A1 Mini and the flagship Bambu Lab X2D.

The A1 Mini is a compact, entry-level FDM printer built for speed and simplicity — a great machine for understanding the fundamentals of slicing, bed leveling, and filament handling without being overwhelmed by features. We went through its full specification sheet in detail, from build volume to supported materials.

At the other end sits the X2D — Bambu Lab's current flagship, built around a Dual-Nozzle Extrusion System and an actively heated chamber designed for premium surface quality and engineering-grade materials. Studying its capabilities gave a real sense of what "industrial-grade" desktop 3D printing looks like in 2026.

A1 Mini vs. X2D — Specification Comparison

🖨 Bambu Lab A1 Mini

The compact entry point into the Bambu Lab ecosystem small footprint, fast prints, and a magnetic flexible build plate.

  • Build Volume: 180 × 180 × 180 mm
  • Max Toolhead Speed: up to 500 mm/s
  • Max Bed Temperature: 80 °C
  • Max Nozzle Temperature: 300 °C
  • Build Plate: Magnetic flexible PEI (textured / smooth)
  • Supported Filaments: PLA, PETG, TPU, PVA

🖨 Bambu Lab X2D

The current flagship of the X-series built for premium surface quality, dual-material workflows, and engineering-grade materials.

  • Build Volume: 256 × 256 × 260 mm (main nozzle) / 235.5 × 256 × 256 mm (dual-nozzle)
  • Extrusion: Dual-nozzle system (main + auxiliary)
  • Chamber: Actively heated, up to 65 °C
  • Max Nozzle Temperature: 300 °C
  • Air Filtration: Three-stage (pre-filter + HEPA + activated carbon)
  • Positioning: Flagship of the X-series, successor to the X1 Carbon
Key Concepts Learned

Magnetic Build Plates

Flexible PEI plates that snap onto the bed magnetically, making print removal as simple as flexing the plate.

Slicing & Bambu Studio

The software layer that converts a 3D model into printable layers, with material-specific presets baked in.

Dual-Nozzle Extrusion

The X2D's main and auxiliary nozzles enable faster color changes and reduced material waste compared to single-nozzle systems.

Active Chamber Heating

A heated chamber reduces warping and improves layer adhesion for engineering-grade filaments.

Learning Outcome:
Understanding the Bambu Lab ecosystem completed the fabrication picture for the week additive (3D printing), subtractive (laser cutting), and cutting-based (vinyl/iron-on) processes all working together. This combination is exactly what's needed to prototype enclosures, fixtures, and mechanical parts for future project work.

GUEST LECTURE — INNOVATION & DESIGN METHODOLOGY

Design Thinking & Rapid Prototyping

One of the standout moments of Week 2 was a guest lecture by Prof. Girija Kumari Sagi, an international business strategist and cross-functional management expert with a strong background in innovation and entrepreneurship mentoring. The session shifted focus — from machines and materials to Methodology: how do you actually go about building something new, the right way?

The core framework introduced was Design Thinking — a five-stage, human-centered approach to problem-solving: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, and Test. Rather than starting with a solution, the framework pushes you to start with the user and the problem — and only then move toward building.

We also explored Rapid Prototyping as a mindset rather than just a set of tools, including a fascinating look at Japanese Origami techniques as a form of structural prototyping — folding flat material into complex, functional 3D forms with minimal waste and no additional fasteners. It was a great reminder that not every prototype needs a machine; sometimes a sheet of paper and the right fold pattern is enough to test an idea.

The Five Stages of Design Thinking

1. Empathize

Understanding the real needs, motivations, and pain points of the people a project is being built for.

2. Define

Translating those observations into a clear, well-scoped problem statement worth solving.

3. Ideate

Generating a wide range of possible solutions before narrowing down to the most promising ones.

4. Prototype

Building quick, low-cost representations of ideas to make them tangible and testable.

5. Test

Putting prototypes in front of real users, gathering feedback, and iterating based on results.

A Personal Highlight:
During the session, I was asked to share details about my own project — and got to talk through the Computer Vision, Embedded Systems, and Open-Source Integration ideas I've been working on. Explaining my project to an industry expert, and hearing their perspective on it, was genuinely one of the most exciting and motivating parts of the week.

LASER CUTTING & MACHINE CONTROL SOFTWARE

LaserCAD & Laser Cutting Workflow

LaserCAD is machine-control software used with Trocen-based CO₂ laser cutters. During this session, the complete workflow from software installation to machine communication and job transfer was explored in detail.

The software provides tools for creating vector-based designs, assigning cutting parameters to individual layers, and sending jobs directly to the laser controller for execution.

The complete workflow covered during the learning session included:

  • Installing LaserCAD V8.28 and configuring the environment
  • Setting up USB communication drivers for machine connectivity
  • Linking CorelDraw integration for design import workflows
  • Understanding the LaserCAD workspace and tool layout
  • Creating basic vector designs using ellipse and text tools
  • Managing Cut and Engrave layers with distinct color assignments
  • Configuring speed, power, and operating mode parameters
  • Establishing COM port communication with the laser controller
  • Downloading completed jobs to the controller memory for execution

A sample nameplate design was created using the ellipse and text tools inside LaserCAD. Different layer colors were assigned to define cutting and engraving operations independently, allowing precise control over how each element is processed by the machine.

The session went beyond theory — once the workspace and parameters were configured, each of us got to take a piece of Cardboard and run our own design through the laser cutter using LaserCAD. Watching a design built on screen turn into a precisely cut physical piece, beam by beam, was a small but genuinely thrilling first taste of digital fabrication.

Learning Outcome:
The session demonstrated how digital designs are translated into machine-readable instructions and highlighted the importance of proper parameter configuration before sending any job to the laser cutter.
Key Concepts Learned

Vector-Based Design

All laser cutting operations require vector paths. LaserCAD uses these paths to define the exact trajectory of the laser beam.

Layer Management

Different colored layers define independent operations. Each layer carries its own speed, power, and mode settings.

Machine Communication

USB-based COM port communication links the computer to the Trocen controller, enabling direct job transfer to machine memory.

Origin & Positioning

Correct origin setup ensures the design is cut at the intended location on the material without misalignment.

Controller-Based Job Transfer

Jobs downloaded to the controller can run independently from the computer, enabling standalone machine operation.

Anatomy of a CO₂ Laser Cutting System

Beyond the software, the session also covered the physical path a laser beam takes from generation to cut — a journey through a precise chain of optical components inside the machine.

CO₂ Laser Tube

The source of the beam itself — a sealed glass tube in which an electrical discharge excites a CO₂ gas mixture to produce the infrared laser beam.

Mirror 1 & Mirror 2

A pair of precisely aligned mirrors redirect the beam from the tube, across the gantry, and down toward the cutting head — any misalignment here directly affects cut quality.

Focus Lens & Nozzle Head

The focus lens concentrates the beam to a fine point at the nozzle head, which also directs a stream of compressed air to clear debris and reduce flare-ups during cutting.

Workpiece & Bed Surface

The final stage — where the focused beam meets the material on the honeycomb bed, cutting or engraving along the vector path defined in LaserCAD.

MATERIAL CONFIGURATION & SAFETY PROTOCOLS

Laser Parameters, Anatomy & Safety

A significant component of the laser cutting module involved understanding how speed, power, and operating mode parameters affect cutting and engraving performance across different materials.

The SIL laser parameter chart was introduced as a reference guide for selecting appropriate settings based on material type and thickness. The chart covered configurations for: acrylic, MDF, cardboard, paper, leather, marble, glass, thermocol, and wood applications.

Cut vs. Engrave — Operating Mode Comparison

✂ Cutting

Used for fully penetrating the material to separate shapes and parts.

  • Lower speed settings
  • Higher power output
  • Complete material separation
  • Sharp, clean edge results

◎ Engraving

Used for surface marking, texturing, and shallow material removal.

  • Higher speed settings
  • Lower power output
  • Surface-level material removal
  • Precise detail and texture
Support Systems — Chiller & Air Compressor

Two support systems run alongside the laser tube on every job, and both were emphasized as essential to safe, clean cutting:

Water Chiller

Continuously circulates coolant through the CO₂ laser tube to keep it within safe operating temperature, preventing overheating and permanent tube damage. It must always be switched on before the laser itself.

Air Compressor

Feeds the air-assist stream through the nozzle head, blowing combustion by-products and debris away from the cut line — reducing flare-ups, scorching, and charred edges, especially on wood and acrylic.

Safety & Maintenance Protocols

The importance of water-chiller operation was emphasized as a critical safety requirement during all laser operations.

  • Always start the water chiller before powering on the laser
  • Perform regular water replacement and quality checks
  • Ensure the air compressor is running to provide air-assist during cutting and engraving
  • Monitor power settings to avoid material scorching or fire risk
  • Select material-specific parameters before any cutting job
  • Never leave the machine unattended during active operation
Learning Outcome:
Understanding parameter selection — and the support systems running behind every job — is critical for achieving accurate cuts, preventing material damage, maintaining consistent output quality, and ensuring machine safety throughout fabrication operations.

VINYL CUTTING & IRON-ON DESIGN FABRICATION

Cricut Design Space & Iron-On Design Workflow

The second fabrication workflow explored during Week 2 involved the Cricut Maker 3 precision cutting machine and Cricut Design Space software. The software acts as the primary interface for preparing vinyl and iron-on projects before sending them to the cutting machine.

This part of the week held a special moment for me — Dr. Kiran Wakchaure sir gave me the first opportunity in the lab to operate the Cricut Maker 3 myself. Getting hands-on with an industry-grade cutting machine on a live project — rather than just observing — made this one of the most memorable sessions of the week.

Cricut Design Space provides a web-based and desktop design environment that enables users to compose layouts, apply text and graphics, preview material placement, and send optimized cut files directly to the cutter — all within a single integrated workflow.

The complete Cricut workflow covered during the session included:

  • Downloading and installing Cricut Design Space desktop application
  • Account setup and new project initialization
  • Using text and image tools on the canvas workspace
  • Preparing and sizing an iron-on vinyl design
  • Applying the mirror function required for iron-on transfers
  • Previewing the design using Cricut's product mockup tools
  • Selecting material type and confirming machine settings
  • Sending the project to Cricut Maker 3 for cutting

Alongside iron-on transfers, the session also covered Adhesive Vinyl — cutting custom stickers and logo decals directly from vector designs, and understanding how cut settings change between adhesive vinyl, iron-on vinyl, and other Smart Materials.

Key Concepts Explored

Design Canvas

A ruler-based workspace for precise design placement, scaling, and alignment before any material is cut.

Iron-On Materials

Material selection within Design Space determines blade pressure, cutting speed, and heat transfer behavior.

Mirror Function

Iron-on designs must be horizontally mirrored before cutting to ensure correct orientation after heat transfer application.

Adhesive Vinyl & Stickers

Custom stickers and logo decals are cut from adhesive vinyl, weeded by hand, and transferred onto surfaces using transfer tape.

Preview Workflow

Cricut's built-in mockup preview allows visualization of the final product appearance before committing to fabrication.

Cricut Maker 3 — Key Features

Adaptive Tool System

A single QuickSwap housing accepts up to 13 different tools — for cutting, scoring, debossing, engraving, and more — without needing separate machines.

300+ Material Compatibility

From thin paper and adhesive vinyl to leather, basswood, and fabric — all cut with the same machine using the right blade and settings.

Speed & Cutting Force

Up to 2× faster cutting than the original Cricut Maker, with significantly higher cutting force than entry-level Explore machines.

Smart Materials — Matless Cutting

Smart Vinyl and Smart Iron-On can be fed directly into the machine without a cutting mat, enabling long, continuous cuts.

Learning Outcome:
The Cricut workflow demonstrated how digital graphics can be efficiently converted into physical custom products through structured design preparation, correct material configuration, and machine-assisted precision cutting — and getting first-hand operating experience made this learning stick in a way that observation alone couldn't.

REFLECTION

Personal Reflection

Week 2 was, without question, one of the richest weeks of the internship so far — not because of any single machine or session, but because of how everything connected. It started with a conversation about what to build, moved through the methodology of how to build it well, and ended with genuinely exciting hands-on time on real industrial machines.

The mentorship session with Dr. Kiran Wakchaure sir and Arvind Mukundan sir reframed how I think about resources — realizing that the lab already contains nearly everything needed to take an idea from raw material to finished product was a real confidence boost going into the rest of the internship. The guest lecture on Design Thinking by Prof. Girija Kumari Sagi added a methodology layer that I plan to apply directly to my own project — starting from empathy and a clear problem definition rather than jumping straight into building.

On the fabrication side, working with LaserCAD deepened my understanding of laser cutting, engraving, parameter optimization, and the optical anatomy of a CO₂ laser system, while the Bambu Lab session introduced 3D printing as the additive piece of the fabrication puzzle. But the personal highlight was getting hands-on with the Cricut Maker 3 for the first time — being trusted to operate it on a live project made the whole week feel like a genuine step from "student" to "maker."

If there's one takeaway from Week 2, it's that successful fabrication is as much about mindset and methodology — empathy, ideation, iteration — as it is about machine parameters, material selection, and strict adherence to safety protocols throughout the production process.
